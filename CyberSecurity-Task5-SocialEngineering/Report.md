# 1. Introduction

Social engineering is a cyberattack technique that relies on manipulating people rather than exploiting technical vulnerabilities. Attackers use deception, trust, fear, or urgency to trick victims into revealing sensitive information or performing actions that compromise security.

Unlike malware or hacking tools, social engineering primarily targets human behavior, making awareness and training essential for defense.

---

# 2. Common Social Engineering Attacks

## 2.1 Phishing

Phishing is a fraudulent attempt to obtain sensitive information through fake emails, websites, or messages.

### Example

A fake bank email asking users to verify their account by clicking a malicious link.

### Prevention

- Verify sender addresses.
- Avoid clicking suspicious links.
- Enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).
- Use spam filters.

---

## 2.2 Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a targeted phishing attack aimed at a specific individual or organization.

### Example

An employee receives an email appearing to come from their manager requesting confidential documents.

### Prevention

- Verify unexpected requests.
- Confirm requests through another communication channel.
- Conduct employee awareness training.

---

## 2.3 Whaling

Whaling targets high-profile individuals such as CEOs, directors, and senior executives.

### Example

A fake legal notice sent to a company CEO requesting urgent action.

### Prevention

- Executive cybersecurity training.
- Strong email verification procedures.
- Multi-level approval for financial transactions.

---

## 2.4 Vishing

Vishing (Voice Phishing) uses phone calls to deceive victims.

### Example

A scammer impersonates a bank representative and asks for OTPs or account details.

### Prevention

- Never share OTPs or PINs over phone calls.
- Verify the caller's identity.
- Contact the organization using official numbers.

---

## 2.5 Smishing

Smishing uses SMS or messaging applications to deliver malicious links or requests.

### Example

A text message claiming that your parcel is delayed and asking you to click a tracking link.

### Prevention

- Avoid clicking unknown links.
- Verify messages through official websites.
- Report suspicious messages.

---

## 2.6 Pretexting

Pretexting involves creating a believable story to convince a victim to reveal confidential information.

### Example

An attacker pretends to be an IT support engineer requesting login credentials.

### Prevention

- Verify identities before sharing information.
- Follow organizational verification procedures.
- Limit disclosure of sensitive data.

---

## 2.7 Baiting

Baiting offers something attractive to lure victims into compromising security.

### Example

An infected USB drive labeled "Confidential Salary Data" left in a public area.

### Prevention

- Never connect unknown USB devices.
- Use endpoint protection software.
- Educate users about removable media risks.

---

## 2.8 Tailgating

Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized individual gains physical access by following an authorized person into a secure area.

### Example

An attacker enters an office by following an employee through a secured entrance.

### Prevention

- Do not allow unknown individuals to enter restricted areas.
- Use access cards and visitor management systems.
- Report suspicious behavior.

---

# 3. Best Practices

- Conduct regular cybersecurity awareness training.
- Verify identities before sharing sensitive information.
- Use Multi-Factor Authentication.
- Keep software updated.
- Report suspicious emails and phone calls.
- Follow the principle of least privilege.

---

# 4. Conclusion

Social engineering attacks exploit human psychology rather than technical weaknesses. By improving user awareness, implementing security policies, and encouraging verification of unexpected requests, individuals and organizations can significantly reduce the risk of successful attacks.
